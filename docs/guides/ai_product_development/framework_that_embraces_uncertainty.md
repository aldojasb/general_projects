# **How I Start ML Projects: With the Business Problem**

In my experience, a common pattern in the data science and data engineering community is that projects often begin with the technology. Teams get caught up in choosing the latest algorithm, framework, or pipeline tool, only to realize months later that the business impact is unclear, resulting in a project that there is not clear business benefits.

In my view, machine learning is not the destination but a step along the way. As Eric Siegel reminds us in *The AI Playbook*, “Do you have an AI strategy?” makes as much sense as asking, “Do we have an Excel strategy?” or "Do we have a Power BI strategy?". In this sense, ML is a tool to achieve outcomes, not the outcome itself.



## **Business First, Technology Second**

From my experience, projects gain much more traction when they are framed as business initiatives. Instead of saying, *AI will improve operations*, I prefer to frame it as, *We will improve operations (using ML)*. The difference is subtle but powerful. The first puts the spotlight on technology. The second puts the spotlight on the business outcome we are trying to achieve.

At Procter & Gamble, I applied this mindset while leading projects, and they generated more than 290 million dollars per year in value. What made the difference was treating machine learning, analytics, and AI as enablers to drive business results, rather than positioning them as the center of the project.



## **Backward Planning: Start From the deployment**

A lesson that has proven valuable in my work, and that Eric Siegel highlights in *The AI Playbook*, is to think about deployment first. Once the business goal is clear, I try to define upfront how the outcomes of the project will actually be used: *Which decision will be improved? How will this change day-to-day operations? In what way will users consume and apply the outputs?* Clarifying these points early has helped me avoid the trap of building models in isolation and instead kept projects measurable and aligned with their intended impact.



## **A Framework for Framing ML Projects**

When I look back at the projects that worked best, they all followed a similar pattern. It is not a rigid recipe, but rather a way I try to guide my own work:

1. **Identify inefficiency or client's pain point**

   What operational problem needs solving? I usually phrase it as: *We will improve X (using ML)*.

2. **Define user stories**

   How will an end user, operator, or decision-maker be assisted by the Aritificial Intelligence (AI), Machine Learning (ML) system? Think through how people will interact with the solution and what will actually change for them.

3. **Establish the deployment goal**

   What value will the AI, ML product add once live in operations? Make the expected value of the AI or ML system explicit before building anything.

4. **Define the Prediction and Metrics**

   Specify the prediction target and the evaluation metrics that will prove success.

5. **Prepare the Data**

   Check the data and prepare it to be consumed.

6. **Build and Test Models**

   Select algorithms, train models, and experiment until the metrics are met.

7. **Launch**

   Roll out the best version into operations, but treat it as the beginning of an iterative process, not the end.



## Closing Thought

From my experience designing and deploying ML projects, I have learned to pause at the beginning and ask three questions:

- Can I clearly state the inefficiency I am fixing?
- Do I know what decision will be improved?
- Do I know how the prediction will actually be used in operations?

If I don’t have clear answers, I know it is too early to move ahead. Spending time to clarify these points has made a big difference in keeping projects aligned with real business value.
